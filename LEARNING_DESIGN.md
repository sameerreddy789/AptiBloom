# Learning design

## Design goal

The student should improve both:

- **mastery:** choosing and executing the right method accurately; and
- **readiness:** doing it accurately under realistic placement time constraints.

These are shown separately. A fast guesser is not treated as a master, and a careful beginner is not punished for learning slowly.

## The BLOOM loop

### B — Begin with a low-stakes attempt

Start a concept with one approachable problem before a lecture. This reveals prior knowledge and creates curiosity. No speed points are awarded.

### L — Learn through a short visual model

Use a diagram, number line, ratio table, highlighted sentence, or step animation to build intuition. Keep direct explanation short and focused on one idea.

### O — Own the method

Ask the learner to complete steps, choose the next operation, estimate an answer, or rearrange information. Avoid passive “next” tapping.

### O — Overcome the misconception

When an answer is wrong, identify the likely mistake, explain the relevant step, then present a near-neighbour problem. The retry is part of learning and earns mastery evidence.

### M — Mix and revisit

End with one previously learned skill. Schedule later retrieval after increasing intervals and mix topics so students must identify the method rather than repeat a visible pattern.

### Prove under placement conditions

After untimed accuracy is stable, introduce timed sprints, sectional tests, and full simulations. This ordering protects understanding while still preparing for the real filter.

## Evidence behind the method

- Roediger and Karpicke reported that retrieval testing improved delayed retention compared with repeated study, despite repeated study increasing confidence.
- Cepeda and colleagues synthesized hundreds of distributed-practice comparisons and found that spacing and retention interval jointly affect later recall.
- Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility techniques; self-explanation and interleaving were promising/moderate.
- Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis found positive effects of gamification on learning outcomes overall, while noting that motivational and behavioral effects were less stable and design/context matter.
- Brilliant's published method applies pretesting, visual interaction, immediate custom feedback, targeted practice, spacing, interleaving, and gradual removal of scaffolding.

The conclusion is not “games automatically teach.” Good instructional content and meaningful practice must exist first; game systems should promote those behaviors.

## Feedback hierarchy

Feedback should answer the smallest useful question:

1. **Outcome:** correct or incorrect.
2. **Location:** which step or interpretation failed.
3. **Reason:** why that step is wrong.
4. **Hint:** the next productive move.
5. **Worked solution:** full method, revealed progressively.
6. **Near-neighbour retry:** a similar but not identical problem.
7. **Transfer:** a differently worded problem requiring the same concept.

Showing the full answer immediately is the fallback, not the default.

## Misconception examples

- Percentages: treating a percentage-point change as percent change.
- Ratios: adding the same number to both sides rather than scaling.
- Ages: mixing present and future variables.
- Time/work: adding completion times rather than work rates.
- Grammar: choosing what sounds familiar instead of checking subject–verb agreement.
- Para jumbles: selecting a sentence with an unresolved pronoun as the opener.
- Syllogisms: treating “some” as “all.”

Each reviewed question should carry one or more misconception tags so feedback and retries can be specific.

## Adaptive challenge

The target is roughly the learner's current boundary: enough success to feel competent, enough difficulty to require thought. A simple rules engine is sufficient initially.

- Two easy first-try answers move toward application questions.
- A wrong answer plus successful guided retry keeps the learner at the concept level but improves confidence.
- Repeated errors trigger a prerequisite lesson, not easier-looking random questions.
- Strong untimed accuracy unlocks speed practice.
- Old skills return through scheduled review even after a topic appears complete.

## Mastery criteria

A topic becomes “Mastered” only when the learner:

- has attempted an adequate variety of item forms;
- reaches at least 80% recent first-attempt accuracy;
- succeeds across more than one session;
- completes at least one spaced review;
- solves transfer/mixed questions without a visible topic label; and
- meets a separate timing target for “Placement Ready.”

Exact thresholds must be calibrated from pilot data; the numbers above are starting rules.

## Calm motivation principles

- Reward returning to a weak skill, not protecting a perfect record.
- Offer a flexible weekly rhythm rather than a guilt-based daily streak.
- Celebrate strategy and improvement, not “genius.”
- Default progress to private.
- Make competition opt-in and match similar ability levels.
- Use cooperative squad goals more often than individual ranking.
- Permit pause, reduced motion, quiet mode, and untimed learning.
